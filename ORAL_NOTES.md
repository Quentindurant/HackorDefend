## WHAT IS THE GAME (~2 min)

> "Hack or Defend is a **card game** for 2 to 4 players. The idea is simple: each round, a cyber attack scenario is shown to all the players. Everyone reads it and tries to find the correct defense. Then the answer is revealed with an explanation.
>
> For example, one card shows this scenario: *'You receive an email from support@paypa1.com asking you to verify your account by clicking a link.'* Players have to say which defense stops this attack. The correct answer is Security Awareness Training — which means training employees to recognize fake emails.
>
> The game has **15 cards** and **3 difficulty levels**:
> - Beginner: hints are shown on the card, no timer
> - Intermediate: no hints, no timer
> - Expert: no hints, and players have only 20 seconds to answer
>
> Each correct answer gives 1 point. The player with the most points at the end wins."

## EDUCATIONAL CHOICES (~3 min)

> "Now I want to explain why we designed the game this way.
>
> **First, why scenarios and not just definitions?**
> We could have just written 'Phishing = fake email'. But that's easy to forget. When you read a realistic story — like a hacker who pretends to be your bank — you can imagine it happening to you. That makes it much easier to remember. This is called contextual learning.
>
> **Second, why show the explanation after the answer?**
> It's not enough to just know the right answer. If you know that 2FA stops a brute force attack, but you don't understand why, you will forget it quickly. The explanation helps players understand the logic, not just memorize.
>
> **Third, why 3 difficulty levels?**
> Because in a real classroom, not everyone has the same level. Some students already know a bit about cybersecurity, others have never heard of SQL injection. With 3 levels, everyone can play. A teacher can also use the game as an introduction with Beginner mode, and then challenge students with Expert mode a few weeks later.
>
> **And why a card game and not just a quiz?**
> Because with a card game, players discuss together. They argue, they explain their choices to each other. When you have to justify your answer to someone else, you learn better. A quiz is individual — this game is social."

## WHY CYBERSECURITY (~1 min)

> "We chose cybersecurity because it's relevant for every digital student, no matter what their specialisation is.
>
> A developer needs to know about SQL injection. A designer needs to understand phishing. A project manager needs to know about ransomware. Even a marketer who handles customer data needs to understand what a data breach is.
>
> Also, if you look at job offers in the digital sector, cybersecurity is mentioned more and more, even for non-technical roles. So it's a real skill that students can use after school."

## Q&A — POSSIBLE QUESTIONS & ANSWERS

*(Prepare these but don't read them — just know them)*

---

**Q: Why did you choose cybersecurity and not another topic?**
> "Because it's a topic that is useful for everyone in digital, not just developers. And we also find it interesting — it's a topic that we wanted to learn more about ourselves. Making the game helped us understand it better too."

---

**Q: How is it different from just doing a Kahoot?**
> "In Kahoot, you play alone and click fast. In our game, players discuss together before answering. You have to argue your choice and listen to others. That's more like a real learning situation. Also, we show the explanation after each answer, which Kahoot doesn't really do."

---

**Q: What happens if a player doesn't know anything about cybersecurity?**
> "That's exactly why we have the Beginner mode with hints. The hint doesn't give the answer directly, but it points you in the right direction. And even if you get it wrong, you still learn because the explanation is shown after."

---

**Q: Can this game be used in class by a teacher?**
> "Yes, that's actually one of our ideas. A teacher could use it at the beginning of a cybersecurity lesson as an introduction, or at the end to check if students understood. The Expert mode with the timer could also be used as a challenge between groups."

---

**Q: Why did you build a web app and not just print the cards?**
> "Printing cards takes time and money. With the app, you just open a link and you can play. Also, the app handles the scores automatically and has the timer for Expert mode, which is hard to do with paper cards. But the game concept works with printed cards too — the app is just more convenient."

---

**Q: Did you test the game with other people?**
> "Yes, we tested it with [classmates / friends — adapt]. The feedback was that the scenarios were realistic and interesting, and that the Expert mode was more stressful than expected — which is good, it means the timer works well as a challenge."

---

**Q: What would you add to the game if you had more time?**
> "We would like to add more cards — maybe 30 or 40 instead of 15. We would also like to add a team mode where two teams compete against each other. And maybe a 'sudden death' mode where one wrong answer and you're out."
